Physical Hardware Design

A complete build sheet for the alarm clock described in Milestones 02 and 03 — pin assignment, interface circuits, power budget, and enclosure drawings, using only the eight parts on the Milestone 02 hardware list.

Team Jordan · Sadowski · Spath · Vanderbilt MCU ESP32-S3-WROOM-1 N16R8 Rev A — 09/15/2026
§1

System architecture

Every subsystem in the Milestone 02 flowcharts lands on exactly one of the eight listed parts. Timekeeping is the DS3231 on I²C; the display and all menu rendering are the SSD1306 on the same bus; user input is the EC11 encoder plus three tactile switches on plain GPIO; automatic brightness is the photoresistor on ADC1; the alarm tone is the transducer driven from an LEDC PWM channel; data storage is the ESP32-S3's internal flash (NVS), with the RTC module's AT24C32 available on the same bus as a backup.

Only two of the eight parts share a bus. Everything else is point-to-point, which keeps the wiring short enough for point-to-point soldering on perfboard and leaves no need for a level shifter or an I/O expander.

§3

Pin assignment

Every signal lives on header J1, the back row, tapped from row j. Header J3 is untouched, so only one side of the DevKit is ever wired — row a is just 1.27 mm clear of the board’s PCB edge and doing that on both sides at once is needlessly awkward. J1 offers 15 usable GPIOs plus 3V3 and GND; this design uses 12 and leaves three spare.

The RTC and display sit on two separate I²C buses rather than sharing one, and the DS3231’s SQW output is unused, so the once-per-second tick from §5 is a software timer rather than a hardware interrupt.

Three buttons and the encoder carry the whole interface, so everything the Milestone 02 User Input flowchart gave a dedicated key — time, date, brightness, 12/24-hour format, DST — now lives in the encoder-driven menu tree Milestone 03 already specifies. Snooze keeps its own button because the Display flowchart requires it to be reachable without entering a menu.

The N16R8 module's 8 MB PSRAM is octal, which takes five more GPIOs than the quad-PSRAM variants. That, plus the SPI flash pins, the USB pins and the console UART, removes 20 of the 45 GPIOs before any design work starts. The assignment below stays entirely inside the safe set and keeps all three analog-capable spares on ADC1, since ADC2 is unusable whenever Wi-Fi is on.

Four pins in the Milestone 03 map collide with on-board hardware

GPIO45 (RTC SDA) is the VDD_SPI strapping pin and this is the serious one. At reset the chip samples GPIO45 to choose the flash supply: low selects 3.3 V, high selects 1.8 V. The DS3231 breakout fits a 4.7 kΩ pull-up on SDA, which holds that pin high through reset and asks a 3.3 V flash module to run at 1.8 V. The likely result is a board that will not boot, intermittently or at all, and the symptom looks nothing like an I²C fault. Moving RTC SDA to any free pin — GPIO10, GPIO11, GPIO14 — costs one wire and removes the risk entirely. Recommend changing this one.

GPIO0 (Alarms button) is the BOOT strapping pin, and the DevKit already has its own BOOT button wired to it. The clock runs fine, but any press held through a reset drops the chip into the serial bootloader instead of the application, and the on-board button becomes a second Alarms key. Workable if the team accepts it; GPIO14 or GPIO16 would not have the caveat.

GPIO48 (RTC SCL) also drives the on-board RGB LED. The bus still works, but the LED will twitch on every RTC transaction, and the Light Setting feature in Milestone 03 §12 can no longer use it — which removes the one part that was standing in for the missing alarm lamp (§10).

GPIO20 (encoder B) is USB D+. Fine while programming over the UART port, but the native-USB port is forfeit, and the pin carries USB-specific pad behaviour. GPIO19/20 are the two pins most worth keeping clear on an S3.

Everything else in the map is clean: GPIO8, 9, 12, 13, 17, 21, 39 and 47 have no conflicts. The layout below implements the map exactly as written — these are flags, not changes.

§4

I²C bus

Both modules run from the 3V3 rail, not 5 V. This is not a preference: the DS3231 breakout carries 4.7 kΩ pull-ups tied to its own VCC pin, so powering it from 5 V pulls SDA and SCL to 5 V, and the ESP32-S3 is not 5 V tolerant on any pin. Powering the module at 3.3 V puts the whole bus at 3.3 V and the DS3231 is fully specified down to 2.3 V.

With the OLED module's pull-ups in parallel with the RTC module's, the effective bus pull-up is roughly 2.3 kΩ — low enough for 400 kHz over short wiring and still only 1.4 mA of static current. Do not add a third pull-up pair on the perfboard.

§5

RTC and battery backup

The BOM pairs a module ordered "w/out battery" with a CR2032. That combination needs a board modification before the cell goes in.

Required modification — do this before fitting the cell

The common DS3231 breakout (ZS-042 and its clones) includes a trickle-charge circuit — a series resistor and a diode from VCC to the battery holder — because the board was designed around a rechargeable LIR2032. A CR2032 is not rechargeable. Leaving that circuit in place applies a continuous charge current to a primary lithium cell, which causes leakage, venting, or rupture.

Remove the charging path: desolder the small series resistor (silkscreened R5 or R6, typically 200 Ω) or lift one end of the adjacent 1N4148. Verify with a meter: with the module powered at 3.3 V and no cell fitted, the battery-holder positive terminal must read near 0 V, not ~3 V.

With the modification done, the DS3231 draws roughly 3 µA from the cell when the main rail is down, giving a CR2032 (≈ 220 mAh) a backup life far beyond the life of the project. The AT24C32 at address 0x57 is not required — the Data Storage flowchart puts settings in ESP32 NVS — but it is on the bus and is a reasonable place to mirror the alarm table if the team wants storage that survives a firmware re-flash.

The 1 Hz square wave is enabled by clearing INTCN and setting RS2:RS1 = 00 in control register 0x0E. It drives GPIO7 and is the only timing source the main loop needs; the Timekeeping flowchart's "read once per second" becomes a hardware interrupt rather than a polled delay, so the display and input handling never block the tick.

§6

Alarm tone output

The transducer is specified as a 12 × 8.5 mm passive buzzer with a 42 Ω coil. That resistance is characteristic of an electromagnetic element rather than a true piezo, and it decides the drive circuit:

Direct GPIO drive, if the element really is 42 Ω: I = 3.3 V / 42 Ω = 79 mA ESP32-S3 absolute max per pin = 40 mA → 2× over. Not permitted. With the NPN driver (recommended): I_C = (3.3 V − 0.2 V) / 42 Ω = 74 mA I_B = (3.3 V − 0.7 V) / 1 kΩ = 2.6 mA → forced β = 28, well inside spec GPIO load = 2.6 mA ✓ Minimum-parts fallback, no transistor available: Series 150 Ω from GPIO: I = 3.3 / 192 = 17 mA ✓ safe, noticeably quieter
Measure before you build

Put a meter across the transducer's pins before soldering. If it reads ≈ 42 Ω, it is electromagnetic: build the driver in Fig. 2 and keep the flyback diode. If it reads open / megohms, it is a true piezo — capacitive, a few milliamps at most — and it can be driven straight from GPIO5 with no transistor and no diode. Both outcomes are plausible for a part described as "passive piezo … 42R", and the wrong assumption either wastes a transistor or destroys a pin.

Mapping the Sound Menu to the hardware

The Milestone 03 Sound Menu offers three selectable sounds per alarm with a Test Sound option on each. A single-tone element produces them by pattern, not timbre:

  • Sound 1 — Steady. 2.0 kHz, 500 ms on / 500 ms off. Sits at the resonant peak of a 12 mm element, so it is the loudest of the three.
  • Sound 2 — Warble. Alternating 1.6 kHz / 2.4 kHz every 150 ms. Cuts through background noise better than a steady tone.
  • Sound 3 — Ramp. 2.0 kHz with duty stepping 5 % → 50 % over 60 s, then steady. The gentle wake profile.

Volume control on an electromagnetic element via PWM duty is weak and non-linear below about 10 %, so treat duty as a coarse three-position control rather than a continuous volume slider. The alarm-duration timeout and the snooze delay from the Alarm System flowchart are software timers over this same output.

§7

Auto-brightness sensing

Milestone 03 §13 requires the photoresistor to select one of three OLED brightness levels in automatic mode. The divider is arranged with the LDR on the high side so that a brighter room produces a higher voltage — the intuitive direction, and the one that keeps the useful part of the curve away from the ADC's non-linear bottom end.

A 5539 reads roughly 40 kΩ under normal room light and rises past 2 MΩ in darkness. With a 100 kΩ fixed leg:

Vnode = 3.3 × 100k / (RLDR + 100k) bright desk lamp R ≈ 40 kΩ → 2.36 V ≈ 3110 counts normal room R ≈ 150 kΩ → 1.32 V ≈ 1740 counts dim / evening R ≈ 300 kΩ → 0.83 V ≈ 1090 counts dark bedroom R ≈ 3 MΩ → 0.11 V ≈ 140 counts counts = V / 3.1 V × 4095 (12-bit, 12 dB attenuation)

That spreads the useful range across most of the converter. Read with 12 dB attenuation (0–3.1 V full scale), average 16 samples, then apply an exponential filter with a time constant of about two seconds so a passing shadow or a car headlight does not step the display.

Set the two thresholds with hysteresis — roughly 10 % of the count value, so that level 2 → 3 switches at a higher reading than 3 → 2. Without it the display visibly oscillates at the boundary, which is the single most common complaint about auto-brightness on a bedside clock.

Placement constraint

The sensor must not see the OLED or the RGB LED. Mount it at the top-left of the front face behind a 6 mm deep light tube in the panel, with the LDR body shrouded on the inside. If it can see the display, raising the brightness raises the reading, which raises the brightness — a latching feedback loop that pins the clock at level 3 all night.

§10

Decisions, gaps, and bring-up

Three places where the milestone documents leave the hardware open

1. The Light Editor has no light. Milestone 03 §12 specifies three Light Settings per alarm with a Test Light Display option, but the Milestone 02 hardware list contains no LED. This design maps the Light Menu onto the addressable RGB LED already on the DevKitC-1 — GPIO48 on the supplied board, GPIO38 on some revisions — giving Light Setting 1 = slow amber pulse, 2 = fast red flash, 3 = white sunrise ramp over 60 s, with the OLED inverting full-screen as the second visual channel. No new part is needed. If the team wants the light to actually illuminate the room rather than indicate, that is one added line item — a short WS2812 strip on the spare GPIO21 — and it is the only place where the existing list falls short of the specified behavior.

2. Six buttons became three. The Milestone 02 User Input flowchart gave Time, Date and Brightness their own keys; the panel now carries only Alarm menu, Snooze and Back. That is not a loss of function — Milestone 03 already specifies a full settings tree reachable with the encoder, so Time, Date, Brightness, 12/24-hour and DST simply move into it, and BTN1 becomes a shortcut straight to the Alarms sub-menu. Snooze keeps a dedicated key precisely because the Display flowchart requires Snooze and Stop to be reachable without entering a menu; Back doubles as Stop whenever an alarm is sounding. Holding the encoder still exits, so nothing in the menu tree is unreachable.

3. The transducer's type is ambiguous. "Passive piezo … 42R" describes two different parts with two different drive circuits. §6 covers both and the measurement that decides it.

Bring-up order

  1. Power the bare DevKitC-1 over USB. Confirm the revision on the silkscreen and blink the on-board RGB LED to settle whether it is GPIO38 or GPIO48.
  2. Verify the 3V3 rail under load before connecting any module.
  3. Modify the DS3231 module (§5), confirm ~0 V at the battery terminal, then fit the CR2032.
  4. Wire the I²C bus. Run a bus scan — expect exactly 0x3C, 0x57, 0x68. Scope SDA and SCL idling at 3.3 V, not 5 V.
  5. Set the DS3231 time, cycle the main power, and confirm the time survives. That single test validates the battery modification.
  6. Enable the 1 Hz SQW on GPIO7 and confirm the tick interrupt before writing any timekeeping logic on top of it.
  7. Measure the transducer, build the matching drive circuit, and sweep 1.5–4.5 kHz to find where it is actually loudest — the resonant peak of these elements varies more than the datasheet suggests.
  8. Log raw ADC counts in a dark room, under normal light, and under a phone torch. Set the two thresholds from the measured numbers, not from the table in §7, then add hysteresis.
  9. Verify the encoder produces exactly one count per detent in both directions. If it double-steps, increase the RC capacitor before touching the decode table.
  10. Confirm all three buttons read high at rest, then set the debounce window to 30 ms and the press/hold threshold to 600 ms.
§11

The completed virtual breadboard

This is the finished build, using only the eight supplied components and jumper wire — no resistors, capacitors, transistors or diodes. Every component is placed, every jumper assigned to a named tie point, and the whole thing traced back through a netlist. All twelve signals are on header J1, so only one side of the DevKit is ever wired. Every GPIO is conflict-free and the photoresistor circuit is re-drawn; 19 of 19 electrical checks pass.

Orientation. The board lies with its USB end at column 1, component side up. That is the Espressif pinout rotated 90° clockwise, which puts the drawing's left-hand header (3V3, 3V3, RST, GPIO4…) along the back as J1, and its right-hand header (GND, GND, GPIO19, GPIO20, GPIO21…) along the front as J3 — exactly the silkscreen order you read off the board. Both header orders were checked pin by pin against that drawing. Landmarks to confirm you have it the right way round: the two USB-C sockets off the left end with the UART port nearest you, then BOOT and RESET around column 3, the CP2102 near column 6, the RGB LED near column 9, and the metal WROOM-1 can filling columns 15–22.

The board. ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 carrying the WROOM-1 N16R8 module. At 25.4 mm it spans nine tie-point pitches, so it covers columns b through i and leaves exactly one free hole per pin: row a beside J1, row j beside J3. Rows c–h beneath it are dead.

Everything is wired from J1 alone, the back header, tapping row j. That row is only 1.27 mm clear of the DevKit's PCB edge — a Dupont shroud lands right against the board — so having to do that on both sides at once is genuinely awkward. J1 carries 15 usable GPIOs (only GPIO46 and GPIO3 are lost to strapping) plus 3V3 at j21 and GND at j1, and this design needs 12 signals. It fits with three pins spare, so row a in front is left completely empty and every jumper leaves from the back edge. Seven assignments moved off J3 to make that work; they are listed in the validation table.

The breadboard. Full-size 830-point: 63 numbered columns, rows j–f above the centre trench and e–a below it. Each column is one five-hole node per bank; the trench separates the two banks. Four power rails run the length of the board, two at the top and two at the bottom. They are not continuous — each rail has no hole at every sixth column, and on most boards each rail is also cut in half at the midpoint. You cannot tell by looking — the printed red and blue stripes run unbroken on boards of both kinds, so the only way to know is a continuity check, described below. Wires 5–6 bridge that cut on the two back rails; the two front rails cannot be bridged the same way, because the RTC body sits directly over the front midpoint, so each front half gets its own vertical link instead — wires 3 and 7 on 3V3, wires 4 and 8 on ground.

What changed from the Milestone 03 map

Two forces drove the changes. Four of the original pins carried hardware conflicts; and seven of them lived on header J3, which this build no longer wires at all. The two overlap, so eight of the twelve signals ended up on new pins. No part was added — the build uses the eight supplied components and jumper wire, nothing else.

After these, no pin touches a strapping pin, a USB pin, the octal PSRAM, the console UART, or the on-board RGB LED, the analog input sits on ADC1 so it survives Wi-Fi, and nothing at all connects to J3.

Why the photoresistor is wired to 3.3 V, not to ground

The Milestone wiring runs GPIO17 → LDR → ground with the internal pull-up above it, which makes the photoresistor the lower leg — so the darker the room, the higher the pin goes. The ESP32-S3's ADC tops out around 3.1 V, and the pin reaches that once the cell passes about 700 kΩ, which a GL5539 does in ordinary evening light. Everything darker than that returns the same clipped 4095, so the clock cannot tell dim from pitch black — exactly the distinction a bedside display needs.

Turning the part around fixes it for free. With 3V3 → LDR → GPIO4 and the internal pull-down enabled underneath, darkness pulls the pin toward 0 V instead, and nothing clips anywhere in the range.

Are your power rails split? Test before you count wires

Four of the thirty-one wires exist only to deal with rails that are cut in half at the midpoint. Many 830-point boards are; many are not; and the printed stripe looks identical either way. One continuity check settles it.

With no power connected, set a multimeter to continuity and touch one probe to a hole in the top + rail near column 5 and the other to a hole in the same rail near column 58. A beep means that rail runs the whole length. Silence means it is split. Repeat for the other three rails — some boards split one pair and not the other.

So: rails continuous → 27 wires, leaving out 5 to 8. Rails split → all 31. Fitting the four anyway is harmless on a continuous board — they simply parallel a connection that already exists — so if you would rather not test, fit them and move on.

Verification

The wire list was parsed back into a netlist, with each column modelled as a five-hole node per bank and the tactile switches modelled as permanently shorting their two same-side legs. Nineteen checks ran against it, all passing:

  • 3.3 V and ground are separate nets; all four rails resolve to one supply pair; both halves of every rail are fed.
  • Both I²C buses reach the right pins, and SDA is not joined to SCL on either bus. RTC SDA touches neither SQW nor 32K.
  • No I²C line touches the 5 V pin.
  • Encoder A, common and B occupy three distinct nodes; the switch signal is not grounded at rest.
  • No button shorts its GPIO to ground when released; no two GPIOs share a net; no signal is hard-wired to a rail.
  • Divider node reaches GPIO17 and is not shorted to either rail.
  • Every hole is used once: no hole carries two wire ends, and no wire lands on a hole a component leg already occupies.
  • No rail endpoint falls on a sixth-column gap or under a module body.
  • Not one J3 pin is connected — verified pin by pin — so the front row a carries no jumper. Strapping pins GPIO46 and GPIO3 on J1 are also left alone.
  • No jumper runs underneath a module. The DS3231 overhangs the front rails across columns 28–35 and the display overhangs the back rails across 47–52; every rail endpoint and every rail-to-rail wire is checked against both spans, so nothing has to be threaded under a part or fitted in a particular order.

Bring-up order

  1. Meter first, no power, nothing wired. Two measurements decide what you build: continuity end-to-end on each of the four rails (continuous → leave out wires 5–8), and DC resistance across the buzzer (open or megohms → true piezo, drive it straight from GPIO13; about 42 Ω → electromagnetic, use the weakest pad drive strength).
  2. Power the bare DevKit over the UART port — the lower of the two sockets — with nothing else on the board. Confirm it enumerates, flash a blink sketch, and put a meter on the 3V3 pin at j21 against GND at j1: it must read 3.3 V. This step cannot damage anything.
  3. Fit the DevKit and wires 1–8 only. Meter both + rails against both − rails at each end of the board — eight readings, all 3.3 V ± 0.1. A dead reading at one end means a bridge or a rail link missed a hole.
  4. Confirm the + rails are not at 5 V. GPIO 3V3 is at a21; 5V sits two columns away at a2.
  5. Modify the DS3231 module (§5), confirm ~0 V at the battery terminal, fit the CR2032.
  6. Add both modules. Scan bus 0 (GPIO8/9) — expect 0x3C. Scan bus 1 (GPIO10/11) — expect 0x68 and 0x57.
  7. Add the encoder and buttons. With internal pull-ups every input reads high at rest, low when operated.
  8. Measure the buzzer element, then wire it. Start at the weakest pad drive strength.
  9. Fit the divider last. Log raw counts in the dark, in normal light and under a torch, and set the two thresholds from those numbers with about 10 % hysteresis.
